English speakers should arrange a China connectivity plan before departure, then decide whether to add a local SIM after arrival. For a short trip, international roaming or a travel eSIM is often the easiest way to get online quickly. For a longer stay, a local Chinese SIM card can be more useful because some services work better with a mainland China phone number.

## At a glance

Have working data before you leave the airport. Use roaming or a travel eSIM if you want the simplest arrival. Buy a local SIM from an official mobile service counter if you need a Chinese phone number, longer-term data, or local app verification. Bring your passport, use official counters, and keep a backup plan for your first day.

## Why internet access matters so much in China

Mobile data is not just convenient in China. It supports maps, translation, ride-hailing, attraction reservations, payment apps, hotel communication, train tickets, and emergency changes.

If your phone does not work on arrival, simple tasks become harder: finding the hotel address, calling a ride, translating a menu, or opening a booking confirmation. Your first goal should be basic reliable data, not the cheapest possible plan.

## Your main options

Most visitors choose one of three routes:

- **International roaming:** easiest if your home carrier offers a reliable China package.
- **Travel eSIM:** convenient for data-only access if your phone supports eSIM.
- **Local Chinese SIM card:** useful for longer stays and services that prefer a mainland China number.

There is no single best choice. The right answer depends on trip length, phone compatibility, budget, and whether you need a local number.

## Option 1: international roaming

Roaming is the least stressful arrival option. You keep your normal phone number and avoid finding a service counter immediately after a long flight.

It is best for:

- Short trips.
- Business travelers who need continuity.
- Visitors who want working data the moment the plane lands.
- Travelers who are not sure whether their phone supports eSIM.

The downside is cost. Check the daily rate, data cap, speed limits, and whether hotspot sharing is allowed.

## Option 2: travel eSIM

A travel eSIM can be a strong choice if your phone supports eSIM and your plan covers mainland China. You can install it before departure and activate it on arrival.

Before buying, check:

- Whether your exact phone model supports eSIM.
- Whether the plan covers mainland China, not only Hong Kong or Macau.
- Whether the plan is data-only or includes calls and SMS.
- Whether hotspot sharing is allowed.
- What happens after the data allowance is used.

Many eSIMs do not provide a mainland China phone number. That may be fine for maps and messaging, but it can be limiting for some local app verification flows.

## Option 3: local Chinese SIM card

A local SIM can be valuable for longer stays, study, work, apartment hunting, and deeper use of Chinese apps. It can also give you a mainland China phone number, which may help with deliveries, property management, local services, and some reservations.

Foreign visitors should buy from official service counters or major carrier stores. Bring your passport. Registration requirements and available packages can vary by city, airport, and carrier.

At Beijing airports, official visitor service information points travelers to SIM card service desks and major operators. In the city, go to a carrier's official store rather than buying from unclear resellers.

## Do you need a Chinese phone number?

For a short sightseeing trip, not always. You can often manage with roaming or eSIM data plus apps such as Alipay, WeChat, DiDi, maps, and translation.

A Chinese number becomes more useful when you need:

- Apartment rental and residence registration communication.
- Food delivery or local services.
- Longer-term banking, school, or work setup.
- Frequent calls with hotels, agents, drivers, or property managers.
- More reliable local SMS verification.

## Airport Wi-Fi and your first hour

Some airports provide Wi-Fi access with passport-based verification or service counters that can help international travelers. Treat airport Wi-Fi as a bridge, not your whole connectivity plan.

Before you land, save offline:

- Hotel name and address in Chinese.
- First-day transport plan.
- Booking confirmations.
- Emergency contact details.
- Screenshots of payment app setup steps.

If airport Wi-Fi fails or verification is confusing, go to an official information counter or mobile service desk.

## Phone compatibility

Before departure, confirm:

- Your phone is unlocked.
- Your phone supports the mobile bands commonly used in China.
- Your phone supports eSIM if you plan to use one.
- Your charger and battery pack are ready for a long arrival day.

If your phone is locked to a carrier, a local SIM may not work. Solve that before departure, not at the airport counter.

## Connectivity and Chinese apps

Some Chinese apps work better after you have stable mobile data and a reliable phone number. Set up the essentials before depending on them in public.

Prioritize:

- Alipay and WeChat Pay setup.
- DiDi access for ride-hailing.
- Translation with offline Chinese support.
- Maps and hotel addresses.
- Train or attraction booking confirmations.

The [China apps guide for English speakers](/guides/china-apps-for-english-speakers/) explains how to use Alipay, WeChat, DiDi, Dianping, and Meituan without assuming every screen will be in English.

## Security and account safety

Connectivity problems can make travelers careless. Keep basic account safety in place:

- Do not share SMS verification codes.
- Do not install apps from unknown links sent by strangers.
- Avoid entering payment credentials on suspicious Wi-Fi pop-ups.
- Use official app stores and official carrier counters.
- Keep your passport and SIM documents safe.

If a process seems unclear, pause and ask an official counter, hotel front desk, school office, employer, or trusted local contact.

## A simple setup plan

For a short first trip:

1. Arrange roaming or eSIM before departure.
2. Install Alipay, WeChat, DiDi, translation, and maps.
3. Save your hotel address in Chinese.
4. Use airport Wi-Fi only as backup.
5. Skip a local SIM unless you need a Chinese number.

For a longer stay:

1. Arrive with roaming or eSIM as a bridge.
2. Buy a local SIM from an official counter.
3. Register with your passport as required.
4. Update important apps with the local number if useful.
5. Keep your original number active if you need foreign bank verification.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Arriving with no data plan and no offline hotel address.
- Buying a plan that covers Hong Kong but not mainland China.
- Assuming every eSIM includes a Chinese phone number.
- Forgetting to unlock your phone.
- Relying on public Wi-Fi for payments and ride-hailing.
- Waiting until you need a verification code to solve phone-number issues.

Good internet access makes the rest of your China trip easier. Choose the simplest arrival plan first, then add a local SIM only if your trip length or local services make it worthwhile.
