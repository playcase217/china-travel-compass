Jianyang, now a district of Nanping, offers a focused two-day journey into Song-era intellectual and material culture. Its story links **Kaoting Academy**, associated with Zhu Xi's later teaching, with the commercial woodblock books once produced around Masha and Chonghua and the black-glazed tea bowls known internationally as Jian ware.

This is deliberately not another Wuyi Mountain itinerary. Base yourself near Jianyang and treat academies, exhibitions, craft spaces, and the protected kiln landscape as different kinds of evidence. Rebuilt halls, archaeological remains, modern workshops, and museum objects should not be described as though they were interchangeable.

## At a glance

- **Best for:** book history, ceramics, tea culture, intellectual history, and slow regional travel.
- **Time needed:** two days in and around Jianyang.
- **Simple plan:** Kaoting and local exhibitions; Jian ware interpretation and an optional verified kiln-area visit.
- **Main planning issue:** sites are dispersed, and workshop or archaeological access may require advance confirmation.
- **Editorial view:** buy less and ask more—provenance, firing method, restoration, and what is genuinely old matter more than dramatic sales language.

## Day one: Kaoting and the culture of books

Begin at **Kaoting Academy** after checking the current entrance, open buildings, exhibitions, and interpretation services. Official Fujian sources identify it as the final academy founded by Zhu Xi and the center associated with the Kaoting school. The visible complex includes reconstructed and improved exhibition spaces, so use labels to separate historical location, later commemoration, and recent rebuilding.

Continue to any officially open display on **Jianyang woodblock printing**. During the Song and Yuan periods, the Masha and Chonghua areas were important commercial book-production centers. A good exhibition should explain carving, paper, ink, printing, binding, distribution, and the difference between an old block, a modern demonstration, and a souvenir print.

End with a riverside walk in a designated public area. Do not add remote “ancient villages” found on social media unless you have confirmed legal access, return transport, and whether residents welcome visitors.

## Day two: read Jian ware carefully

Use the second day for an official museum, exhibition center, or reputable workshop explaining **Jian ware**. The dark, iron-rich bowls became closely tied to Song tea culture; surviving glaze effects depend on materials and kiln conditions, not simply decorative painting. Look for displays that distinguish excavated historical pieces from contemporary revival work.

The protected **Jian kiln site** extends around Houjing and Chizhong villages near Shuiji. It is an archaeological landscape, not an open quarry for souvenir hunting. Visit only if the responsible authority confirms the public entrance, hours, transport, and permitted route. Never collect sherds, walk onto excavation surfaces, or enter kilns or work areas without permission.

If you buy a contemporary bowl, ask for the maker's name, materials, firing method, care instructions, and a clear receipt. “Imperial,” “museum grade,” or “Song original” are extraordinary claims requiring extraordinary documentation. Export rules can apply to genuine antiquities, so do not buy an alleged archaeological object.

## Practical planning

- Search Nanpingshi, Jianyang, and the exact station on [China Railway 12306](https://www.12306.cn/en/); similar place names can produce costly mistakes.
- Save 建阳区, 考亭书院, 建本, 建窑遗址, and 水吉镇 in Chinese.
- Arrange a licensed car or confirmed local transfer for dispersed sites; do not assume app-based cars will wait in villages.
- Northern Fujian rain can affect roads and outdoor heritage access. Check the [China Meteorological Administration](https://www.cma.gov.cn/en/).
- Confirm workshop visits directly and ask before photographing craftspeople, formulas, kilns, or private workspaces.
