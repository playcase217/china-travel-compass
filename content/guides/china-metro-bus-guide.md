Metros are usually the simplest way to cross a large Chinese city; buses are valuable for the final connection and for places without rail. Prepare a working phone, Chinese destination names, and at least one payment backup, then verify the local operator's current fare and service rules.

## At a glance

- **Metro default:** buy a single-journey ticket or use a locally supported transit code or card.
- **Bus default:** confirm route number and direction, and carry small RMB notes or coins as a backup where cash is accepted.
- **Do not assume:** one city's QR code, transit card, bank-card gate, or transfer rule will work in another.
- **Navigation habit:** save the station, line, direction, exit number, bus stop, and destination in Chinese.

## Set up two ways to pay

China's official foreign-visitor guide lists single-journey metro tickets, transit cards, mobile payment apps, and—in some systems—bank cards as possible options. For buses it lists RMB cash, cards, and supported mobile codes. Actual acceptance is local.

Use the [China mobile payment guide](/guides/china-mobile-payment-guide/) to prepare a wallet, but keep a physical fallback. A phone can lose power, an identity check can fail, a foreign card can be declined, or a city-specific code may not activate. Where a bus accepts cash, exact small change is safer because change may not be available.

## Taking the metro

Search for the line number or name, destination direction, interchange station, and exit—not merely the nearest station pin. Large interchanges may involve long corridors, and different exits can emerge on opposite sides of a major road or complex.

With a single-journey ticket, keep it until you exit. With a QR code or contactless method, use the same method at entry and exit unless the operator says otherwise. If the gate rejects a passport-linked product or payment, go to the staffed service point instead of repeatedly forcing the gate.

Allow extra time around the morning and evening commute, major events, and public holidays. Security screening and crowd controls vary by system and station.

## Taking a city bus

Match the route number, stop name, and travel direction. Two stops with similar names may sit on different sides of a divided road. Show the Chinese destination to staff or another passenger only when safe to do so, and follow your progress on a map without blocking the door.

Some routes charge a flat fare; others use distance or require a second scan when leaving. Airport buses, suburban routes, tourist lines, and intercity coaches may use separate tickets and are not automatically part of the urban network. Confirm the rule displayed on the vehicle or by the operator.

Signal and boarding customs differ. Stand visibly at the correct stop, let passengers exit, move away from the doors, and keep bags from occupying seats during crowded periods.

## Accessibility, luggage, and late travel

Elevators, step-free transfers, toilets, and staffed entrances are not equally convenient at every station. Travelers using wheelchairs, mobility aids, or strollers should consult the local operator for an accessible route and allow time for staff assistance.

Avoid rush hour with large luggage when possible. Check last-service information through the local operator on the day; do not build a late-night return around a timetable screenshot. Keep enough battery and money for a licensed taxi or app-based ride if the final connection is missed.

## A repeatable city routine

1. Save hotel and destination names in Chinese.
2. Check the official metro map or bus operator information.
3. Screenshot the route, direction, interchange, and exit.
4. Activate the local payment method while online.
5. Keep cash or another accepted payment method ready.
6. Recheck the last service before an evening outing.
