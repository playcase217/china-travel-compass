Longquan is a focused craft destination in the mountains of southwest Zhejiang. Its celadon is not merely a green souvenir style: UNESCO recognizes the traditional firing technology as living intangible cultural heritage. Two days allow a museum-led introduction, one transparent workshop visit, and time to understand why clay, glaze, kiln control, and skilled judgment all matter.

## At a glance

- **Best for:** ceramics, design, craft history, museums, and slower regional travel.
- **Time needed:** two days after reaching Longquan.
- **Simple plan:** museum and town orientation; then one verified workshop, kiln, or celadon heritage venue.
- **Main planning issue:** Longquan, Lishui, Shangyang, historic kiln sites, and rural studios are not one walkable cluster.
- **Editorial view:** watch one process carefully and ask informed questions rather than shopping through many showrooms.

## Day one: build a celadon vocabulary

Begin at a confirmed-open public museum or official celadon exhibition. Learn to distinguish vessel shape, body, glaze color, surface texture, firing, restoration, and date before thinking about purchases. UNESCO's inscription concerns traditional firing knowledge and its transmission; it is not a blanket certificate for every green-glazed object sold in town.

Walk through Longquan's public center and visit a reputable gallery if time allows. Labels such as “master,” “wood-fired,” “handmade,” or “heritage” should lead to questions: who made the piece, where was it fired, what process was used, and is documentation included? Buy because you understand the object, not because a sales story implies guaranteed investment value.

## Day two: one responsible workshop visit

Arrange a workshop, kiln, or heritage-center visit directly with a recognized venue. Shangyang is associated with modern celadon production and interpretation, but opening arrangements and demonstrations can change. Confirm whether the experience is observation, hands-on forming, glazing, firing, or simply retail; a piece shaped today may require drying and later firing rather than immediate collection.

Keep clear of working kilns, raw-material areas, shelves, and tools unless invited. High temperatures, dust, sharp fragments, and industrial equipment are real hazards. Ask before photographing people, recipes, studio layouts, or unfinished work, because technical knowledge and designs may be sensitive.

## Practical planning

- Check current rail services and station names on [China Railway 12306](https://www.12306.cn/en/).
- Group venues by geography and arrange licensed road transport for rural sites.
- Carry your passport and confirm museum closure days, workshop appointments, language support, and payment terms.
- If shipping ceramics, agree in writing on packing, insurance, customs description, tracking, and responsibility for breakage.
- Do not remove shards or material from archaeological kiln sites.
- Check the [China Meteorological Administration](https://www.cma.gov.cn/en/) for mountain rain, heat, cold, and road disruptions.
